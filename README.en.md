# Easy-reading-papers

**English** | [简体中文](README.md)

Reading papers usually involves three questions: is this relevant to what I'm doing, is it worth the time, and will I be able to find it again later.

This skill connects the three. It **starts with a cheap preview** so you can decide whether the paper matches what you need. If you choose to read it, you get a **14-section deep read**. Then the report is **filed by topic, saved as a .docx report, and added to an index**. When you find similar papers later, they can be added to the same topic.

Up to five papers per batch. PDFs, DOIs, arXiv links, and web pages all work.

## 🔍 Deciding whether to read

Each paper gets a preview: the old problem, what this paper solves, how it solves it, and what the results look like. The first three need **three to five sentences** with concrete details — where existing methods get stuck, which modules and mechanisms this paper uses. Results need **at least one key number or comparison**, not a handful of keywords.

This pass reads only the abstract, introduction, related work, and conclusion. If that is all it has, it says "abstract only, not checked against the full text."

Rankings are checked when the papers arrive, not patched in later. STEM and medicine use the 2025 CAS table. Chinese humanities and social science journals use CSSCI and the Peking University core list. English humanities use SSCI/A&HCI. CS conferences use the CCF list, 7th edition, A/B/C. If a venue is not found, it says so; non-CS conferences are marked N/A.

After the preview you choose: full read, method only, experiments only, save for later, or skip. It will not start a deep read without your choice.

"Save for later" and "skip" skip the purpose question and produce no report.

## 📖 Deep read

Once you decide to read, it asks the purpose: deep read, reproduce, find novelty, find a research gap, write a review. The purpose changes the emphasis of the report.

<p align="center"><img src="docs/images/flow-choose.png" width="680"></p>

The report has **14 sections**:

1. Basics: title, authors, venue, DOI, ranking
2. One-sentence conclusion
3. Full abstract, plus two problems and two solutions
4. Framework and pipeline, with what each step does and why
5. Two to five key formulas, each with a symbol table
6. Contributions: old problem, new idea, what it solves
7. Experiments: data, setup, comparisons, ablations
8. Full conclusion, including the authors' stated limitations
9. Claim–Evidence table
10. Critical review: validity problems, strongest rebuttal, avoided points, most likely failure in reproduction
11. Reproducibility (materials and citations for humanities)
12. Where it sits in related work
13. Takeaways and actionable items
14. Open questions

Appendix: evidence index with page, section, and short quotes.

Abstracts and conclusions are included in full. English abstracts are translated into Chinese by default, with names and technical terms kept in English.

Humanities papers skip reproducibility and code. Section 11 becomes "materials and citations", and section 10 drops the reproduction-failure item.

Formulas are LaTeX in the intermediate draft. The report export renders them as images, so the .docx never shows the LaTeX source.

Figures are extracted from the PDF into `figures/` and placed after the relevant paragraph, followed by one line on what the figure shows and how to read it. If extraction fails, the report says so.

Method-only and experiments-only reads are available. Both keep a cross-check: a method read still checks whether the assumptions were tested, and an experiments read still checks whether the experiments support the claimed contribution.

## 💾 Saving the report

Reports go to `papers/` in the current directory by default. It asks once whether you want a different location.

Before saving, it **classifies the paper** instead of dropping it into a folder:

- It reads `_topics.md` and `_index.md`, then shows two or three candidate topics, each with the closest paper already in the library.
- You pick a candidate, create a topic, use `_inbox`, or skip saving.
- A single paper is saved directly. With two or more, it asks whether to save them separately, merged, or both.
- A merged report adds a cross-paper comparison; without it, merged files are just stacked.

The layout:

```
papers/
  _index.md
  _topics.md
  _inbox/
  <NN-Chinese topic>/
    _topic.md
    <year-first author-Chinese short name>/
      report.docx
      figures/
```

Folders use Chinese names: `<NN>-<Chinese topic>` for topics and `<year>-<first author>-<Chinese short name>` for papers. The index keeps a lowercase English `paper_id` as the unique key.

Each paper keeps **only a .docx report**. **Markdown is a temporary conversion source** and is deleted after a successful export. Formulas are images in the report. Layout: body in FangSong GB2312 at 14pt, title at 22pt, English in Times New Roman, first-line indent of two characters, table text centered, and a yellow marker background on the one-sentence conclusion and key numbers.

After saving you get the absolute path, the files created, and the index changes.

## ➕ Adding more papers later

Send the new paper as usual: PDF, DOI, or arXiv. The flow stays the same: preview, your choice, deep read, save.

Before saving it **reads the existing index**:

- It checks for duplicates by DOI, arXiv ID, or title + year + first author.
- If a matching topic exists, it offers candidates so you decide whether to add to it or create a new one.
- A second read of the same paper (for example, method only) is stored as v2, **without overwriting the first**.
- If that topic has a merged report, the new paper is appended and the cross-paper comparison is regenerated.
- The index date is updated and the old reports stay.

A topic can therefore grow over time: start with one or two papers, add related work as you find it, and end up with a small library of your own.

<a id="rules"></a>

## 📏 Rules

- No deep read before the user has picked.
- The abstract is not the full-text conclusion.
- Rankings, page numbers, and figures are never written from memory.
- Saving without a path or an index update is not allowed.
- No more than five papers per batch.

## ⚠️ Limitations

- **Scanned PDFs**: the file opens, but each page is an image with no text layer. Abstract, formulas, page numbers, and text inside figures cannot be extracted, so it reports that no text was available. Run OCR first (Acrobat, ABBYY, Tesseract, etc.), or use a text-based PDF.
- **DOI only**: the preview uses Crossref and publisher metadata plus the abstract. That is enough to judge the general topic, but it is not a full read. A deep read needs open-access full text; if there is none, it asks for the PDF instead of bypassing a paywall. Abstract-only reports are marked "abstract only, not checked against the full text" and do not include page numbers, figure numbers, or specific experiment values.
- **Rankings**: looked up online each time. STEM and medicine use the 2025 CAS table; Chinese humanities use CSSCI and the Peking University core list; English humanities use SSCI/A&HCI; CS conferences use the CCF list, 7th edition. With no network, no reliable match, or a mismatched ISSN, it says so instead of guessing. Rankings change, so the value is the one at lookup time.
- **Information inside figures**: text-only models can read captions and prose, but not curves, axes, or values inside a figure. When a trend depends on the image, the report says it is unverified and asks you to check the figure or provide the data.
- **Formulas and tables**: when PDF parsing fails, the report says the formula or table could not be parsed instead of filling it in from memory.

## ❓ FAQ

**Q: My model can't read images. Can I still use it?**

Yes, with a caveat. Text, abstract translation, figure cropping, layout, and export all work. What it cannot do is read inside figures — where a confusion matrix is wrong, whether a curve converges, which block in a method diagram maps to which module. It will say "I can't see the figure" instead of inventing a conclusion. Use a multimodal model, or send the key figure to a vision model separately and paste the answer back.

**Q: My field has no dataset. What then?**

Humanities, theory, and review papers skip the dataset section. Section 5 becomes key claims, section 7 becomes materials and argument, and section 11 becomes materials and citations. No code or reproducibility section.

**Q: I only have a DOI, or the paper is paywalled. What happens?**

The preview uses Crossref metadata and the abstract so you can judge whether it is worth reading. A deep read needs open-access full text; if there is none, it asks for the PDF instead of bypassing the paywall. Abstract-only reports are marked as such.

**Q: What about scanned PDFs?**

Run OCR first, or use a text-based PDF. Without a text layer it can only report "no text available" and will not fake a report.

**Q: Why is there only a .docx after saving, no Markdown?**

The .docx is the report for reading. Markdown is a temporary conversion source, deleted after a successful export so the two do not drift apart.

**Q: What if the ranking lookup fails?**

It writes "not found". Chinese journals show core-list status (CSSCI, Peking University core, etc.) rather than a zone.

**Q: How do I add similar papers to an existing topic later?**

Send the new paper as usual. On save it reads the index, checks for duplicates, and offers candidate topics; you can add to an existing one or create a new one. A merged report regenerates the cross-paper comparison.
